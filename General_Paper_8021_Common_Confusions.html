What is General Paper?

General Paper is a Cambridge AS Level course where you write about the world. Not one subject — everything. Science, society, politics, the arts, technology, ethics. It rewards students who read widely, think critically, and write clearly.

The good news: you are not tested on facts. You are tested on what you do with ideas. Two exams. One essay. One reading response. That is the whole course.

The Three Assessment Objectives (AOs)

Every mark on every paper connects to one of three AOs. Understanding them is your first real advantage.

AO1 — Selection and Application (28%)Showing you know relevant information and can pick examples that actually support your point. Not everything you know — only what fits.
AO2 — Analysis and Evaluation (37%) — the highest-value skillGoing beyond the surface. Asking: so what? Why does this matter? What is the other side? This is where most marks are won or lost.
AO3 — Written Communication (35%)Clear sentences. Organised paragraphs. Appropriate register. No need for fancy words — need for precise ones.

Three Topic Areas

Paper 1 questions come from these three broad topic buckets. Build knowledge in each one.

1
Economic, Historical, Moral, Political and Social — government, justice, human rights, globalisation, education, work, migration, inequality, religion, family.
2
Science, Environment and Technology — medical ethics, space, privacy, climate, food security, AI, transport, public health, mathematics.
3
Literature, Language, Arts and Media — books, journalism, advertising, censorship, digital media, visual arts, performing arts, heritage.
Reality check: You do not need to be an expert in all of these. You need to have an opinion about them and know a few real examples. Start by picking one topic per area that genuinely interests you and read about it for 10 minutes a week.

How to Choose Your Question (4 Minutes Well Spent)

Students lose marks before they write a single word by choosing the wrong question. Use this process every time.

1
Read all 10 questions once. Do not skip this. It takes 2 minutes and you might find a better option than your first instinct.
2
Ask: can I name at least 3 real examples? Not vague ideas — actual events, places, people, data, or research. If you cannot, move on.
3
Ask: can I argue both sides? Cambridge rewards balance. If you can only think of one perspective, pick a different question.
4
Commit and plan before writing. Two minutes of planning produces a better essay than two minutes of extra writing.

The Essay Structure That Earns Marks

General Paper essays are structured arguments. Every paragraph has a job.

Introduction
(60–80 words)		Define the key term or issue. State your position clearly. Signal what your essay will cover. Do not waste space summarising what everyone already knows.
Body 1
(100–120 words)		Point: State the argument in one sentence. Evidence: Give a real-world example with specifics. Analysis: Explain why the example proves your point. Connect — do not just describe.
Body 2
(100–120 words)		Same structure. Different point. Move your argument forward — do not repeat Body 1 in different words.
Counter-argument
(100–120 words)		Acknowledge the other side — "Critics argue..." or "It is true that..." Then refute or qualify it — explain why your position still holds.
Conclusion
(60–80 words)		Return to your position. Do not introduce new ideas. Synthesise — show how your arguments together prove your point.

Before and After: See the Difference

Question: "Social media has done more harm than good. Discuss."

Weak version: Social media is harmful. Lots of people use it and it causes many problems. Depression is one example. Young people are affected the most and this is a big issue in today's society.
Strong version: Research conducted by the Royal Society for Public Health in the UK found that Instagram ranked as the most harmful social media platform for young people's mental health, contributing to anxiety, depression, and poor body image. This is significant because it demonstrates that the harm is not incidental — it is structural. Platforms designed around likes and comparisons produce measurable psychological damage, regardless of individual user intentions.
What changed? The strong version names a real source, gives a specific finding, and — most importantly — analyses what the finding means. That last sentence is AO2. That is where marks live.

Section A vs. Section B

Section A

Explain, interpret, and respond to the material. Short answers plus one extended response (up to 120 words) where you write persuasively — expressing and justifying a view.

Section B

Detailed language analysis. How does word choice, tone, and sentence structure work? You identify inference, analyse style, and evaluate strengths and weaknesses of the writing.

Section B warning: This is where most students underperform. They summarise instead of analyse. "The writer uses the word 'devastating'" is not analysis. "The word 'devastating' is emotionally charged — it positions the reader to feel grief rather than neutral concern" is analysis.

Reading Strategy: The 3-Pass Method

1
First pass — 2 minutes. Read the whole passage for general understanding. Do not stop at words you do not know. Get the big picture: what is this about? What position does the writer take?
2
Second pass — read the questions. Before you dive back in, read every question. Now you know exactly what you are looking for.
3
Third pass — targeted. Go back and annotate. Circle key words. Mark shifts in argument. Note tone. Now answer with evidence in hand, not from memory.

Language Analysis: Word → Effect → Purpose

When asked how language works, use this structure every time.

Example extract: "The relentless destruction of the Amazon is nothing short of a crime against humanity."
W
Word/Phrase: "relentless" and "crime against humanity"
E
Effect: "Relentless" implies the destruction never pauses, removing any sense of hope or human agency. "Crime against humanity" borrows legal language associated with war crimes — it escalates the severity far beyond environmental concern.
P
Purpose: The writer is not simply informing — they are engineering outrage. By framing deforestation as criminal, they demand a moral response from the reader rather than an intellectual one.

Practice: Analyse This Extract

"Year after year, city after city, the same tired promises emerge from politicians who have mastered the art of appearing concerned while doing absolutely nothing."

Command Words: The Decoder Ring

Cambridge uses specific words to tell you exactly what kind of response they want. Misreading a command word costs marks before you write anything.

Command WordWhat it means
AnalyseBreak it down. Show how the parts relate. Go below the surface.
AssessMake a judgement. Weigh the evidence. Reach a supported conclusion.
CompareIdentify similarities and/or differences. Always link the two items — do not describe one, then the other.
ConsiderReview and respond to given information thoughtfully.
ContrastIdentify and comment on differences only.
DemonstrateShow how, or give an example.
DescribeState the points. Give characteristics and main features. Do not analyse unless separately asked.
DevelopTake forward to a more advanced stage. Build upon given information.
DiscussWrite about issues in depth in a structured way. Multiple sides. Do not just list.
EvaluateJudge the quality, importance, amount, or value of something. Do not just describe.
ExamineInvestigate closely, in detail. Go deeper than a summary.
ExplainSet out purposes or reasons. Make relationships clear. Say why and/or how with evidence.
IdentifyName, select, or recognise. No extended explanation needed unless separately asked.
JustifySupport a case with evidence and argument. Not just opinion.
SuggestApply knowledge to propose ideas. Multiple valid responses exist. Show your reasoning.
SummariseSelect and present the main points only. No detail. No analysis. Be selective.

The "To What Extent" Trap

This phrase appears often and trips students up. It is not asking for yes or no. It asks for a nuanced position on a spectrum.

Weak response: I completely agree that social media is harmful. There are many reasons for this...
Strong response: While social media creates measurable harm in specific contexts — particularly for adolescent mental health — a blanket claim of harm overstates the case. Its impact depends on the platform, the user's age, and access to digital literacy education. A more accurate position is that social media's harms are real but preventable, and the degree of harm is largely a policy failure rather than an inherent feature of the technology.
Key move: Take a position — but qualify it. Cambridge rewards students who show they understand complexity, not students who shout loudest.

Practice: Rewrite the Opening

Prompt: "Discuss the role of the internet in modern education."
Weak opening: "The internet is very important in education. Students use it every day."

Sentence Starters That Actually Work

These phrases are scaffolding. Use them until you do not need them.

Introducing your argument

  • It is worth noting that...
  • A key consideration here is...
  • The evidence strongly suggests that...
  • One of the most significant factors is...

Adding evidence

  • This is illustrated by the case of...
  • Research conducted by [source] found that...
  • A notable example is...
  • Statistics from [context] show that...

Analysing your evidence (AO2 — most valuable)

  • This is significant because it demonstrates that...
  • What this reveals is...
  • The implications of this are considerable — it suggests that...
  • This goes beyond [surface claim] — at its core, it reflects...

Introducing counter-arguments

  • Critics of this view would argue that...
  • It is true that [concede], however...
  • While this argument has merit, it overlooks...
  • The counter-position — that [...] — fails to account for...

What Strong General Paper Students Do Differently

  • They read news regularly — BBC, The Guardian, The Economist, Reuters — because every article is a potential example bank.
  • They do not memorise facts. They build arguments. There is a big difference.
  • They always know the other side. For every position they hold, they can articulate the strongest counter-argument.
  • They practise timed writing. The exam is not open-note. Speed and clarity under pressure are trainable skills.
  • They ask "so what?" about everything. Describing a fact is not an argument. The analysis that follows the fact is where marks live.

Mode-Shifting Challenges

These exercises train a different mode of thinking — the kind General Paper rewards.

Devil's Advocate drill: Take a position you personally agree with. Now argue the opposite — as convincingly as you can. Write 5 sentences. This trains you to find counter-arguments before examiners do.
The 5-minute expert: Pick a topic you know nothing about. Read one article. Write a 3-sentence summary of the issue and one argument for and against.
The So What? chain: Take any fact — e.g., global temperatures rose 1.1°C since pre-industrial times. Ask "So what?" four times, going deeper each time. This is how AO2 thinking works.
2-minute argument sprint: A friend gives you a random topic. You have 2 minutes to write the opening paragraph. No research. Build the habit of forming an argument under pressure.
