📝 Explanation:
Community: all populations of species in an area. Ecosystem: community + abiotic factors (water, temperature, soil). Both concepts are important—communities show biotic structure, ecosystems show function.

💡 Hint:
Remember the 10% rule: each level captures ~10% of the energy from the level below.
📝 Explanation:
Energy loss at each step is why food chains don't extend beyond 4-5 levels. Of 1,000 energy units in grass: grasshoppers get ~100, birds get ~10, hawks get ~1. The rest is lost as heat and used for metabolism.

Photosynthesis captures solar energy in glucose. Respiration breaks down glucose to release that energy as ATP for cellular processes (growth, movement, metabolism). Both are continuous—photosynthesis during light, respiration constantly.

Carrying capacity (~50 fish) is determined by available food, oxygen, and space. At 100 fish, these resources are insufficient, so starvation/disease increase and population drops. At 50, resources are sufficient to sustain the population indefinitely.

💡 Hint:
Density-dependent: effect increases with population density (disease spreads faster when animals are crowded). Density-independent: random effect regardless of density.
📝 Explanation:
Disease is density-dependent—it spreads faster in crowded populations (50% killed). Hurricane is density-independent—it kills ~30% regardless of whether population is 1,000 or 10,000 (random damage). Density-dependent factors self-regulate populations; density-independent factors destabilize them.
13 A plant adapted to desert conditions (small leaves, deep roots, waxy cuticle) is introduced to a temperate rainforest (warm, wet year-round). Predict its likely outcome and explain why. Tier 3: Analysis

It will compete equally with native plants and establish a stable population
💡 Hint:
Desert adaptations minimize water loss (good for dry) but prevent efficient water uptake (bad for wet). High humidity promotes fungal growth.
📝 Explanation:
Desert plant adaptations are specialized for low-water environments. In a wet rainforest: (1) Small leaves and waxy cuticle reduce water uptake, starving the plant. (2) Dense, humid conditions promote mold/fungal infection. (3) Competition from efficient rainforest plants is intense. The plant's adaptations are liabilities in a rainforest—it can't thrive.
14 Why do ecosystems with the same biotic diversity (species composition) but different energy inputs from the sun support different-sized populations of apex predators (top carnivores)? Tier 3: Analysis

Energy input determines total primary productivity, which limits energy available up the food chain; higher energy input supports larger apex predator populations
Apex predators have different reproduction rates in different ecosystems
Apex predator populations are unrelated to ecosystem energy input
💡 Hint:
More solar energy captured by producers → more food available throughout the food chain → larger populations at every level.
📝 Explanation:
Energy constrains populations at all trophic levels. A tropical forest (high solar input, high photosynthesis) supports more biomass than a boreal forest (low solar input, lower photosynthesis). Even with identical species composition, the tropical forest's greater primary productivity supports larger apex predator populations because more energy is available as it flows up the food chain.
15 Two identical ponds are created in the same region. Pond A remains undisturbed. Pond B is drained and refilled every 2 years. After 10 years, why might Pond A have higher biodiversity than Pond B? Tier 3: Analysis
Disturbance prevents biodiversity; stable conditions favor pioneer species only
Disturbance resets succession, preventing climax community establishment; Pond B cycles through early succession repeatedly while Pond A reaches a diverse climax community
Disturbance increases biodiversity; Pond B should have higher diversity
Biodiversity is determined by climate, not disturbance
💡 Hint:
Succession takes time. Frequent disturbance prevents the system from reaching a stable, diverse climax state.
📝 Explanation:
Pond A undergoes succession undisturbed: early colonizers → competitors → climax community with maximum diversity. Pond B is reset every 2 years, restarting succession. It never reaches climax; it cycles through pioneer stages repeatedly. Repeated disturbance prevents diversity accumulation. Intermediate disturbance promotes diversity; severe/frequent disturbance prevents it.

16 Biodiversity refers to: Tier 1: Vocabulary
The number of animals in an ecosystem
The variety of life at all levels—genetic, species, and ecosystem diversity
Only the number of plant species in an area
The total amount of biomass in an ecosystem
💡 Hint:
Think about variation at three levels: within species (genetic), among species, and among ecosystems.
📝 Explanation:
Biodiversity has three components: (1) Genetic diversity within species, (2) Species diversity (number of species), (3) Ecosystem diversity (variety of ecosystem types). High biodiversity means high variation at all three levels.
17 What does it mean for a species to be endemic? Tier 1: Vocabulary
It is found in many countries worldwide
It is found naturally in only one geographic location
It is extinct in the wild but exists in zoos
It was recently introduced by humans
💡 Hint:
Endemic = one location only. If the species' habitat is destroyed, it goes globally extinct.
📝 Explanation:
Endemic species exist nowhere else on Earth. Losing their habitat = global extinction. Island endemics (Galápagos tortoises, Hawaiian birds) are especially vulnerable—they have nowhere else to go.
18 An invasive species is best described as: Tier 1: Vocabulary
Any non-native species in an ecosystem
A non-native species that spreads rapidly and causes harm to the ecosystem or human interests
A species that has lived in an area for less than 100 years
A species that outcompetes native species through superior intelligence
💡 Hint:
Invasive = non-native AND harmful. Not all non-natives are invasive; most coexist harmlessly.
📝 Explanation:
Invasive species cause ecological or economic harm. They often lack natural predators, outcompete natives, introduce diseases, or alter habitats. Zebra mussels clog pipes; Asian carp displace native fish; invasive plants reduce fire intervals.
19 A keystone species is one that: Tier 1: Vocabulary
Is the most abundant species in an ecosystem
Has an ecological impact disproportionately large relative to its abundance
Is the largest species in an ecosystem
Provides food for all other species
💡 Hint:
Keystone species are influential, not necessarily numerous. Removing them causes ecosystem collapse.
📝 Explanation:
Sea otters are rare but keystone—they control sea urchin populations. Without otters, urchins overgraze kelp forests, and the ecosystem collapses. One species' removal cascades through the entire system.
20 What does genetic diversity measure? Tier 1: Vocabulary
The number of different species in an area
The variation in genetic material within a population or species
The total number of genes in an ecosystem
How different ecosystems are from each other
💡 Hint:
Genetic diversity = variation within a species. Many variants = adaptable population.
📝 Explanation:
Genetic diversity is variation in genes within a species or population. High genetic diversity means many allele variants, making populations adaptable to change. Low diversity means limited variation, increasing extinction risk from disease or environmental change.
21 A forest habitat of 10,000 acres is divided by roads into five 2,000-acre patches. Why might the species that lived in the continuous forest struggle in these fragmented patches? Tier 2: Application
Total habitat area is sufficient; fragmentation has no effect
Fragmented patches have smaller populations, reduced genetic diversity through drift, and isolation prevents recolonization; species are vulnerable to extinction
Species are too small to survive in patches; they need more than 10,000 acres
Fragmentation increases biodiversity by creating new niches
💡 Hint:
Smaller populations = fewer breeding options = genetic isolation = reduced diversity = higher extinction risk.
📝 Explanation:
Fragmentation harms species even if total area is unchanged. Each 2,000-acre patch has fewer individuals than the 10,000-acre continuous forest. Fewer individuals = genetic drift, inbreeding, reduced adaptation ability. Patches are isolated; if one local population goes extinct, the patch isn't recolonized. Fragmentation is nearly as harmful as habitat loss.
22 A species hunted to 30 individuals is protected and the population recovers to 3,000 today. Why might genetic analysis show unusually low genetic diversity despite population recovery? Tier 2: Application
Recovery is too recent; genetic diversity will eventually increase
The population bottleneck (reduction to 30) caused loss of genetic variants that can't be recovered; diversity is permanently reduced even if population size recovers
Large population sizes automatically reduce genetic diversity
Genetic diversity is unrelated to population size
💡 Hint:
When population shrinks, alleles are randomly lost. Once lost, they're gone forever—recovery in numbers doesn't restore lost variation.
📝 Explanation:
A genetic bottleneck causes permanent diversity loss. If only 30 individuals survive, alleles present only in the dead individuals are lost forever. The recovered population (3,000) all descend from those 30, so genetic variation can never return to pre-bottleneck levels. Northern elephant seals (bottlenecked to ~20) recovered numerically but remain genetically uniform.

Invasive species cause documented harm. Zebra mussels cause economic damage (clogged pipes, millions in losses) and ecological damage (outcompete natives, alter ecosystems). They're invasive because of their harmful impact, not just because they're non-native.

Rainforests produce the most oxygen on Earth
Rainforests prevent climate change
💡 Hint:
Efficiency: protect a hotspot with 1,000 species; protect a temperate forest with 200 species. Where can you conserve more diversity with same resources?
📝 Explanation:
Hotspots (high-diversity, high-endemism areas) are conservation priorities because protecting them protects a huge proportion of global biodiversity. Many hotspot species are found nowhere else, so losing hotspots means losing species that can't be protected elsewhere.
25 Two populations of the same species are separated by an impassable mountain range. Over many generations, the populations diverge genetically despite no selection pressure. What genetic process explains this? Tier 2: Application
Gene flow homogenizes them
Genetic drift causes random changes in isolated populations; without gene flow to introduce variation, alleles are randomly lost in each population
Mutation stops in isolated populations
Selection pressures must be different; genetic change requires selection
💡 Hint:
Isolation prevents gene flow. Without gene flow (migration), each population independently loses alleles by chance alone.
📝 Explanation:
Genetic drift is strongest in small, isolated populations. No gene flow between populations means no new alleles arrive from the other population. Each population independently loses alleles randomly. After many generations, the populations diverge genetically even without selection pressure—just from random drift accumulation.

💡 Hint:
The original population has low genetic diversity (small number). A bottleneck caused allele loss that can't be recovered by growing the original population. Botanic gardens preserve the genetic diversity from the original wild source.

27 A non-native fish species introduced to a lake is thriving and reproducing, but native fish populations remain stable. 💡 Hint:

29 Region A has diverse ecosystems (rainforest, wetland, grassland, coral reef). Region B has only one ecosystem type (grassland). If climate change shifts temperature and precipitation, which region is more likely to maintain food security for its human population, and why? Tier 3: Analysis

💡 Hint:
